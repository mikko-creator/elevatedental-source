What this field covers

Find yours on this list

Your orthodontist takes records, reads how the upper and lower teeth meet, and sets a sequence of movements that brings them into a stable relationship.

  • Crowded or overlapping teeth. There is not enough arch for the teeth to sit side by side.
  • Gaps and spacing. The opposite problem, and it responds to the same appliances.
  • Protruding upper front teeth. Often the complaint that brings an adult in.
  • Overbite, underbite and crossbite. A relationship problem between the arches rather than within one of them.
  • An open bite. The front teeth do not meet when the back teeth do.
  • Drift after an extraction. Neighbouring teeth lean into a space that was left open.
  • Relapse. Treated years ago, retainers stopped, and the teeth have moved back.
What a long case runs into

Alignment cases frequently need one of the other fields

Crowding can hide decay that has to be dealt with before brackets go on. A crossbite can involve a tooth that needs taking out first. Adult cases often finish with restorative work once the teeth are where they belong. Each of those handovers happens inside this practice, on the same records, without your plan going on hold while you find someone else.

What actually decides it

Distance of travel, not preference

Preference is a real input and it is not the deciding one. How far each tooth has to move, and in which direction, is what narrows the choice before anyone asks what you would like.

Trays are on the table when Shorter travel

The movements are moderate and you will keep to the hours

Mild to moderate crowding or spacing, an arch that needs tipping rather than bodily repositioning, and a person whose day allows a tray to stay in for the wear time their orthodontist specifies. Being removable is the advantage and the risk in the same sentence.

Brackets are the answer when Longer travel

Roots have to be moved, or the arches have to be related

Severe crowding, a tooth that has to be uprighted through bone, a crossbite, or a correction that needs continuous force nothing can be taken out of. A fixed appliance also removes compliance from the equation entirely, which for some patients is the deciding factor on its own.

What settles it

Records first, opinion second. The examination, the images and the digital scan show your orthodontist the roots and the arch relationship, and the appliance recommendation follows from those. If both are viable for your case you will be told so plainly, and then preference is a legitimate tie-breaker.

05
The part nobody asks about first

Three questions about time

  1. How long will mine take. Both routes are measured in months. Your orthodontist confirms the length of your case after records and a scan, because it is set by how far your teeth have to travel and not by which appliance you chose.
  2. What makes a case longer. Roots that have to move through bone rather than tips that have to rotate. An arch relationship that has to change rather than teeth that have to straighten within one. A tray sequence worn for fewer hours than it was planned for. Missed adjustment appointments, which stall a fixed case in exactly the same way.
  3. What happens at the end. Teeth move back if nothing holds them, which is why relapse appears on the condition list above. Retention is part of the plan, not an afterthought bought separately, and it is discussed before treatment begins rather than on the day the appliance comes off.

No typical treatment-duration range is published for either appliance. Publishing a borrowed industry average would be a promise this practice has not made, so duration is given case by case.

Whether retainers are included in the treatment fee, and what a replacement costs, is not stated here. It is a question every patient asks at the end of a long course of treatment.

Starting prices

Two floors, and what lifts them

A full course of braces starts at ₱90,000, a representative market figure, and a clear aligner course at ₱180,000, read the same way and not an Elevate quote.

Both are floors rather than quotes. Cases in this field vary more than in most, because the fee follows the number of movements your teeth need and the months you spend in treatment. Three things move it:

  • How many teeth actually have to move, and whether they move within one arch or between two.
  • How long the case runs, because time in treatment is appointments, and appointments are the fee.
  • What has to happen first, an extraction, a filling or gum treatment before an appliance can be fitted at all.

Your exact figure is confirmed at the consultation, in writing, before a single appointment is scheduled.

Before you commit to months

Find out which route your bite actually needs

A consultation here starts with records, and the recommendation comes out of them rather than out of a conversation about what you would prefer.

  1. Records. An examination, images and a digital scan, taken in one visit.
  2. The reading. Your orthodontist reads the roots and the arch relationship out of those records rather than out of what a mirror shows you.
  3. The recommendation. Which appliance suits the case, what the sequence looks like, and how long it is likely to run.
  4. Your decision. Made with the plan in front of you, and nothing is fitted before you agree to it.

Nothing in that sequence commits you to anything. You leave with a written plan, a figure and a length, and the decision to start is a separate appointment you make afterwards.

Whether the first consultation carries a fee is not stated on this page, and no complimentary visit is claimed.
