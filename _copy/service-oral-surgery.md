## §2. What it is

Oral surgery is the part of dentistry that involves cutting. A simple extraction lifts a tooth out whole with forceps. A surgical extraction is what happens when that is not possible: the gum is lifted as a flap, bone is removed where it blocks the path, the tooth is sectioned into pieces, each piece is taken out separately, and the site is cleaned and closed with sutures.

The procedures grouped here are the ones an oral surgeon handles rather than a general dentist.

Impacted wisdom teeth, where a third molar is angled into bone or into the tooth in front of it. Retained roots left behind by an old fracture or an incomplete extraction. Apicoectomy, where the infected tip of a root is removed surgically after root canal treatment has not resolved the infection. Exposure and bonding of an impacted canine so the orthodontist can bring it into the arch. Socket preservation and bone grafting, which rebuild the ridge after a tooth is removed so an implant can be placed later. Frenectomy, which releases a tight lip or tongue attachment. Removal of cysts and soft-tissue lesions, with biopsy where the tissue needs to be examined.

The problem all of these solve is the same one. Something in the jaw is infected, obstructed, painful, or in the way of a restoration that cannot proceed until it is dealt with.

## §3. Who it suits

You need oral surgery if you have an impacted or partially erupted wisdom tooth that keeps getting infected, a tooth broken below the gum line, a root fragment left in the bone, a failing root canal that has not settled after retreatment, an impacted canine that orthodontics needs uncovered, or a site that needs bone before an implant can be placed.

Not every painful tooth is a surgical case, and this is where a specialist assessment saves you a procedure.

A wisdom tooth that is fully erupted, upright, cleanable and causing no symptoms does not automatically need removing. Prophylactic removal of an asymptomatic, functional third molar is not a default here. If the scan shows no pathology and you can clean it, you will be told to keep it and monitor it.

A tooth that can be saved should be saved. An extraction is quicker and costs less today than root canal treatment plus a crown. It is also a permanent loss, and replacing a lost tooth later costs more than the treatment that would have kept it. If your case is restorable, the endodontist or the prosthodontist who could restore it works in the same practice and can look at it before anything is removed.

Some cases belong in a hospital rather than in a dental clinic. A bleeding disorder, current anticoagulant therapy that cannot be interrupted, a history of antiresorptive or bisphosphonate medication, recent cardiac events, immunosuppression, or a case that genuinely requires general anaesthesia all change where treatment should safely happen. Our clinics work under local anaesthesia. If your medical history means your procedure needs a hospital setting, you will be told that at the assessment rather than partway through it.

Tell us your full medical history and every medication and supplement you take, including blood thinners, before the procedure is booked. This is the single piece of information that most often changes the plan.

## §4. How it works

**1. Consultation and imaging, 45 to 60 minutes.** Examination, medical history, and a 3D CBCT scan where the case needs one. For a lower wisdom tooth, that scan shows the exact relationship between the roots and the inferior alveolar nerve canal. For an upper one, it shows how close the roots sit to the sinus floor. This is the information that decides the surgical approach, and it is why the scan comes before the plan.

**2. Planning and medical review, same visit or within a week.** The surgeon decides the approach, whether the tooth will be sectioned, whether bone will be removed, and whether the site will be grafted. Medications are reviewed. You are given the fee in writing and the post-operative instructions in advance, so you can arrange the day around them.

**3. The procedure, 30 to 90 minutes.** Local anaesthesia first, and the site is tested before anything begins. The flap is raised, the tooth or lesion is removed, the site is irrigated and cleaned, a graft material is placed if the plan calls for one, and the flap is sutured. You should feel pressure and movement but not sharpness. Say so immediately if you do, and more anaesthetic is given.

**4. Immediate recovery, 30 to 45 minutes.** You bite on gauze in the clinic until the initial bleeding stops. You leave with written instructions and any prescribed medication.

**5. The first 48 hours.** Swelling peaks around day two. Ice on and off for the first day, rest, soft cold food, no rinsing and no spitting for the first 24 hours, no smoking, and no drinking through a straw.

**6. Review and suture removal, 7 to 10 days, 15 to 20 minutes.** The site is checked for healing and the sutures are removed if they are not the dissolving type.

**7. Onward treatment, where it applies.** A grafted site needs 3 to 6 months before an implant can be placed into it. An exposed canine is passed back to the orthodontist. A biopsy result is discussed with you as soon as it comes back.

## §5. Who performs it

Surgical procedures are performed by an oral surgery specialist. The scan reading, the surgical decision and the procedure itself are theirs.

This matters most at the point where a case is not straightforward. If the CBCT scan shows a root wrapped around the nerve canal, the decision about whether to take the whole tooth or leave a fragment deliberately in place is a surgical judgement, not a general one. If the tooth is restorable after all, the endodontist is in the same practice. If it is coming out and needs replacing, the prosthodontist who will restore the site is too.

## §6. The technology behind it

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics, which means your scan is taken where you are being treated rather than at a separate imaging centre on a separate day. For surgical extraction it is the difference between knowing where the nerve canal runs and estimating it from a flat film.

**Piezotome ultrasonic instrumentation.** A Piezotome cuts bone with ultrasonic vibration rather than with a rotating bur. The frequency is tuned to mineralised tissue, so it cuts bone while being far less likely to tear soft tissue on contact: nerves, the sinus membrane, the gum flap. In practice that means bone can be removed close to a nerve canal or under a sinus floor with a narrower margin of error, and the cut surface is left cleaner for healing.

**Sol Dental Laser.** Our clinics operate a Sol Dental Laser for soft-tissue work. Whether it is used in your procedure depends on what the procedure involves, and the surgeon will tell you at the planning stage.

We do not offer sedation dentistry, and we do not claim to. Procedures here are done under local anaesthesia. If your case needs more than that, it needs a different setting and you will be told so.

Three things move the final figure.

**Case complexity.** A retained root near the surface and a fully impacted horizontal lower third molar sitting on the nerve canal are separated by an hour of surgery and a different level of risk.

**Materials.** A graft material, a membrane, or a suture type chosen for a specific closure all carry their own cost, and they are listed separately rather than folded in silently.

**Number of sites.** Four wisdom teeth removed in one appointment is priced differently from one, and doing them in one visit usually means one recovery period rather than four.

The exact figure is confirmed at consultation, after the scan has been read. It is given to you in writing before the procedure is booked, and it states whether imaging, medication and the review appointment are included.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Will I be awake?**
Yes. Procedures here are done under local anaesthesia, so you are awake and the area is numb. You will feel pressure and movement. You should not feel sharpness, and if you do, tell the surgeon immediately and more anaesthetic is given before work continues.

**How much does it hurt afterwards?**
Expect soreness and swelling that peak around 48 hours and then settle over the following three to five days. Prescribed analgesia is taken on schedule rather than waited for. Most people find day three noticeably easier than day two. Pain that increases after day three, rather than decreasing, is a reason to call the clinic.

**How long is the recovery?**
Most people take one to two days away from work for a surgical extraction and return to normal eating over about a week. Soft tissue closes in one to two weeks. The bone underneath continues remodelling for months, which is why an implant into a grafted site waits three to six months.

**What is dry socket and how do I avoid it?**
Dry socket is the loss of the blood clot from the extraction site, exposing bone, and it is sharply painful around day three to five. You reduce the risk by not smoking, not rinsing or spitting for the first 24 hours, not drinking through a straw, and not disturbing the site with your tongue. If it happens, call the clinic. It is treatable and it is treated the same day where possible.

**Can I drive myself home?**
Under local anaesthesia, generally yes, assuming you feel steady. If you have been given anything that affects alertness, arrange for someone to take you.

**Do I have to take out all four wisdom teeth?**
No. Each one is judged on the scan and on your symptoms. A wisdom tooth that is upright, fully erupted, cleanable and causing no problems can stay and be monitored. Removing teeth that do not need removing is not the approach here.

**What are the risks?**
Every surgical procedure carries them, and they are explained before you consent, not after. For lower wisdom teeth the specific ones are temporary or, less commonly, lasting altered sensation in the lip, chin or tongue if the nerve is disturbed, along with bleeding, infection, dry socket, and sinus communication for upper molars. The CBCT scan exists precisely to quantify the nerve risk in your case in advance rather than discovering it during the procedure.

**When can I go back to the gym?**
Not for the first 48 hours, and build back gradually over the following week. Raising your blood pressure early restarts bleeding.

**Aftercare.** Bite firmly on the gauze for 30 to 45 minutes. No rinsing, spitting, smoking or straws for 24 hours. Ice on and off for the first day, then warm salt-water rinses from day two, five or six times daily. Soft, cool food. Take prescribed medication exactly as directed and finish any antibiotic course. Sleep with your head raised on the first night. Keep the review appointment even if the site feels fine.

**Closing.** The first consultation is free. You will be examined, scanned where the case calls for it, told exactly what the scan shows about your own anatomy, and given the figure for your procedure in writing before anything is booked.
