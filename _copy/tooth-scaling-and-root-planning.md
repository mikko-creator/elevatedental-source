## §2. What it is

Gum disease begins as a soft bacterial film at the gum margin. Left there, it hardens into calculus, the gum detaches from the tooth, and a pocket forms. Inside that pocket the deposit sits on the root surface, out of reach of a toothbrush, and the body responds by breaking down the bone that holds the tooth. That bone does not come back on its own.

Scaling and root planing is the treatment for that pocket. Scaling removes the calculus and the biofilm from the tooth and the root. Root planing smooths the root surface that the deposit was attached to, so the gum tissue can reattach against it and the pocket can shrink. It is carried out under local anaesthetic, usually one quadrant or one half of the mouth at a time, and it is measured before and after so the result is a number rather than an impression.

This is not the same treatment as a routine oral prophylaxis. A prophylaxis cleans above and just below the gum margin in a healthy mouth. Scaling and root planing is a periodontal treatment that works several millimetres deeper, inside a pocket that has already formed.

## §3. Who it suits

You are likely to need it if your gums bleed when you brush or floss, if they are red and swollen, if your breath has changed, if a tooth has become loose or has drifted, or if the gums have receded and the teeth look longer than they used to. It is confirmed by periodontal charting, where the depth of each pocket is measured around every tooth, and by radiographs that show the bone level. Pockets deeper than about three millimetres with bleeding are the usual threshold for treatment.

It is also the treatment that comes first when other work is planned. Crowns, bridges, implants and orthodontics all rest on the gum and bone, so periodontal disease is stabilised before those are started.

**Where scaling and root planing is not enough on its own.** If your gums are inflamed but no bone has been lost, that is gingivitis, and a routine prophylaxis with a change in your daily cleaning will usually resolve it. Where pockets are deep, or where the bone has been lost in a pattern that instruments cannot reach into, scaling and root planing will improve the situation without closing the pocket, and periodontal surgery, regenerative treatment or grafting is then the honest next step. A tooth with very advanced bone loss and mobility may not be savable at all, and we will say so rather than treat around it.

Two things outside the clinic change the outcome more than anything we do in it. Smoking reduces the response of the tissue and masks the bleeding that would otherwise warn you. Uncontrolled diabetes does the same. If either applies to you, say so at the consultation, because it changes the plan and the recall interval.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and periodontal assessment, 45 to 60 minutes.** Full-mouth periodontal charting, with pocket depths recorded at six points around every tooth, plus bleeding scores, mobility, recession and radiographs of the bone level. This is the baseline everything is measured against. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Oral hygiene instruction, 15 to 20 minutes, at the same visit.** Which interdental brush size fits which space, and how to use it. The treatment does not hold without this part, so it is taught rather than mentioned.
3. **Treatment, one to four visits of 45 to 90 minutes.** Local anaesthetic is given for the area being treated, then the root surfaces in each pocket are cleaned and smoothed with ultrasonic and hand instruments. The mouth is usually treated in quadrants or halves so each area gets adequate time.
4. **Interim review, at four to six weeks.** The tissue is checked for early healing and any remaining deposit is dealt with.
5. **Re-evaluation, at six to eight weeks, 45 minutes.** The full charting is repeated and compared with the baseline. This is where the treatment is judged: which pockets have closed, which have improved, and which need something more.
6. **Maintenance recall, every three to four months.** Periodontal disease is controlled rather than cured, and the interval between maintenance visits is the single strongest predictor of whether it stays controlled.

Most cases run to two to four treatment visits over two to six weeks, followed by the re-evaluation. The plan you receive at consultation states the number of visits for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**AirFlow (guided biofilm therapy).** A controlled jet of warm water, air and a fine erythritol powder that lifts bacterial biofilm and staining from the tooth, from the root surface inside the pocket, and from around implants and orthodontic attachments. It reaches into contours a hand instrument cannot follow, and it is gentler on the root surface than repeated hand scaling.

**Piezotome.** A piezoelectric ultrasonic unit. Its motion is used to break up hardened calculus efficiently on the root surface, and in periodontal surgery the same technology works on bone while leaving soft tissue intact.

**Sol Dental Laser.** Used as an adjunct in the treated pocket to decontaminate the soft tissue wall, alongside the mechanical cleaning rather than instead of it.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. Used where the pattern of bone loss matters to the decision: a defect around one root, a furcation between the roots of a molar, or an assessment of whether the site can take an implant if the tooth cannot be kept.

## §8. Results

This treatment is judged by measurement, which is unusual in dentistry and useful to you. Your baseline chart records a pocket depth and a bleeding score at six points around every tooth. At the re-evaluation six to eight weeks later, the same points are measured again and the two charts are compared in front of you.

What good looks like: pockets that have reduced, bleeding scores that have fallen, and firmer tissue that no longer bleeds when you clean between the teeth. What it does not look like: bone that has grown back. Scaling and root planing stops further loss and lets the tissue reattach. It does not rebuild what has already gone, and any page that tells you otherwise is selling something.

Expect the gums to shrink slightly as the swelling resolves. That can leave a little more root exposed and the teeth looking longer, and it can leave small spaces between them. That is healed tissue rather than a complication, and it is worth knowing before it happens.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Will it hurt?**
Local anaesthetic is given for each area before treatment, so the cleaning itself is done on a numb quadrant. Afterwards the gums are usually tender for a day or two and the teeth can be sensitive to cold for a few weeks while the tissue heals. Ordinary painkillers taken as directed are enough for most patients.

**How is this different from a regular cleaning?**
A routine oral prophylaxis cleans the visible surfaces and just under the gum margin in a healthy mouth, and takes one short appointment. Scaling and root planing works inside pockets that have already formed, on root surfaces below the gum, under anaesthetic, over several appointments, and it is measured before and after.

**Why does it take more than one visit?**
Cleaning a pocket properly takes time, and doing the whole mouth in one sitting would mean either rushing it or anaesthetising everything at once. Treating in quadrants or halves gives each area the time it needs.

**Will my teeth feel loose afterwards?**
Teeth can feel slightly different once the swelling settles, because inflamed tissue was holding them snugly. Genuine mobility is assessed at the re-evaluation and is a finding to act on, not something to wait out. Tell your periodontist if a tooth is moving.

**Will the sensitivity go away?**
Usually. Exposed root surface is sensitive while it settles, and it commonly improves over two to six weeks. A desensitising toothpaste used consistently helps, and a fluoride application at the clinic can be added if it persists.

**Do I need antibiotics?**
Not routinely. The deposit on the root is a physical film, so removing it mechanically is the treatment. Antibiotics or antiseptic rinses are used selectively, in aggressive cases or alongside surgery, and are prescribed rather than assumed.

**How often will I need maintenance afterwards?**
Every three to four months for most patients, at least until the tissue has been stable for a sustained period. The interval is set to be shorter than the time the bacterial film needs to re-establish in a pocket. Stretching it back to a routine six-month recall is the most common reason treated gum disease returns.

**What do I need to do at home?**
Brush twice a day for two minutes at the gum margin, and clean between every tooth once a day with the interdental brush sizes you were given. Interdental cleaning matters more here than in any other treatment on this site, because the spaces between the teeth are where the disease lives. If you smoke, stopping does more for the outcome than anything else on this list.
