## §2. What it is

A crown covers the whole visible part of a tooth. The tooth is reduced by a controlled amount on every surface, a copy of it is sent to the laboratory, and the crown is cemented over what remains. Once it is on, the crown takes the biting force that the weakened tooth could no longer take safely.

Material is chosen for the position and the load. Zirconia is the strongest of the tooth-coloured options and is the usual choice for molars and for patients who grind. Lithium disilicate, sold as Emax, is the usual choice at the front, because it lets light through the way enamel does and is easier to match to the neighbouring teeth. Porcelain fused to metal and gold remain valid in particular cases. Your prosthodontist will name the material for your case and the reason for it before the tooth is prepared.

The problem a crown solves is a tooth that is still yours but is no longer strong enough to be left as it is. A large old filling, a crack, a fractured cusp, heavy wear, or the hollowed-out structure left after root canal treatment all put a tooth at risk of splitting. A crown is what prevents the split, and a split root usually cannot be saved.

## §3. Who it suits

A crown suits you if a tooth is heavily broken down, cracked, worn, or has just had root canal treatment, particularly a back tooth that does the chewing. It also suits a tooth that will act as the support for a bridge, and it is the standard restoration placed on top of a dental implant.

**Where a crown is not the right answer.** If the damage is small, a crown removes more healthy tooth than the problem justifies, and a composite filling, an inlay or an onlay is the more conservative treatment. If too little tooth structure remains above the gum, a crown has nothing to grip, and the honest options are a post and core if the root is sound, or extraction and replacement if it is not. If the tooth has a vertical root fracture, or bone loss from advanced gum disease, a crown will not rescue it, and we will tell you that at the assessment rather than after you have paid for one. Active decay and untreated gum inflammation are always treated before a crown is planned.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and assessment, 45 to 60 minutes.** Clinical examination, radiographs, and a check of how much sound tooth remains, the state of the nerve and the condition of the gum. Material and shade are discussed. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Preliminary treatment, where needed.** Removal of decay, replacement of a failing filling, a core build-up, or root canal treatment. Quoted separately and completed before the crown is started.
3. **Preparation and records, 60 to 90 minutes.** The tooth is numbed and shaped. The arch is scanned or impressed, the bite is recorded, and the shade is matched under controlled light.
4. **Temporary crown, same appointment.** A provisional crown protects the prepared tooth and keeps the gum in the right position while the laboratory works.
5. **Laboratory fabrication, one to two weeks.** The crown is made to the scan, the bite record and the shade prescription.
6. **Fitting, 45 minutes.** Fit at the margin, contact with the neighbouring teeth, shade and bite are checked before cementation. Anything that is not right is corrected or the crown is remade rather than cemented as it is.
7. **Review, one short visit two to four weeks later.** The bite is rechecked in use and the gum margin is assessed.

Most cases complete in two appointments across one to two weeks. A tooth that needs root canal treatment or a core build-up first takes longer, and the plan you receive at consultation states the sequence for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**iTero intraoral scanner.** A digital scan of the prepared tooth, the opposing arch and the bite, taken in the chair. It removes the impression tray for most cases, shows the preparation magnified on screen so an undercut is caught before you leave, and sends the file to the laboratory the same day.

**OccluSense bite analysis.** A crown that sits a fraction high is the most common reason a newly restored tooth aches. OccluSense records where each contact lands and how hard, in sequence, so the bite is adjusted against a reading rather than by feel alone.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. Used where a two-dimensional radiograph cannot answer the question: the root anatomy before root canal treatment, a suspected crack, the extent of bone support, or planning a crown on an implant.

Our clinics run eight named systems. These three are the ones that touch a crown case.

## §8. Results

A crown is assessed before it is cemented. At the fitting appointment the margin is checked with a probe and on a radiograph where the edge sits below the gum, the contacts with the neighbouring teeth are checked with floss, the shade is checked in daylight rather than under the chair light, and the bite is read. A crown that does not pass those checks goes back to the laboratory.

The second assessment is at the review, two to four weeks later. By then the gum has settled around the new margin and you have chewed on the tooth, which is when a contact that is a fraction high reveals itself.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Will it hurt?**
The preparation appointment is carried out under local anaesthetic. A tooth with a live nerve is commonly sensitive to cold for a few days afterwards while the temporary crown is in place, and that usually settles once the definitive crown is cemented. Sensitivity that is getting worse rather than better is worth a call, not a wait.

**How long does the temporary crown stay on?**
One to two weeks. It is cemented lightly on purpose so it can be removed without damaging the preparation. Avoid sticky and very hard food on that side, and if it comes off, keep it and call the clinic rather than leaving the tooth uncovered.

**How long does a crown last?**
Longevity depends on the bite, the amount of sound tooth left underneath, your hygiene and whether you grind. Crowns most often fail at the tooth rather than in the porcelain, through decay at the margin or a fracture in the root, which is why the margin is where cleaning matters most.

**Can a crowned tooth still decay?**
Yes, at the margin where the crown meets the tooth. The crown itself cannot decay, but the tooth under it can. Floss or use an interdental brush at that margin every day, and keep your recall appointments so the edge is checked.

**Does a crown need root canal treatment first?**
Not usually. Root canal treatment is done when the nerve is already infected or irreversibly inflamed, not because a crown is planned. If your tooth needs both, the root canal treatment comes first and the crown follows.

**Can a crown be whitened or its shade changed later?**
No. Zirconia and porcelain do not respond to whitening agents. If you intend to whiten your natural teeth, do that first, let the shade settle for about two weeks, then match the crown to the result.

**Do I need a night guard?**
If you grind or clench, yes, we will recommend one. Grinding is the most common cause of a fractured crown, and a guard is a smaller investment than a remake.

**What is aftercare in the first week?**
Chew on the other side for the first day, brush normally including the gum margin, and floss carefully by pulling the floss out sideways rather than snapping it up past a new contact.
