## §2. What it is

A complete denture replaces every tooth in one jaw. The base is a gum-coloured acrylic shaped to sit against your ridge, and the teeth are set into it in the positions your own teeth once held. An upper denture holds by suction across the palate. A lower denture rests on the ridge and is held by the muscles of the tongue and cheeks, which is why the two behave differently in the mouth.

Two routes exist. A conventional denture is made after the extraction sites have healed, usually eight to twelve weeks, so the fit is taken against settled tissue. An immediate denture is made in advance and inserted on the day the teeth come out, so you are not without teeth, then relined once the ridge has finished changing shape.

The problem it solves is functional before it is cosmetic. Missing a full arch changes what you can chew, how you sound on certain consonants, and how the lower third of your face is supported. A denture restores all three, and it can be adjusted, relined and remade as the ridge changes over the years.

## §3. Who it suits

Complete dentures suit you if every tooth in an arch is missing, or if the remaining teeth are beyond restoring and are due for extraction. They also suit patients who cannot have implants placed, whether because of bone volume, a medical condition, or a decision not to have surgery.

They are a reasonable first step even when implants are the eventual goal. A denture can be worn while bone grafts heal, then converted or replaced later.

**Where a complete denture is not the right answer.** If you still have sound, restorable teeth in the arch, keeping them is almost always the better outcome, and a removable partial denture or a bridge is the correct treatment instead. If your priority is a replacement that does not move at all, a conventional lower denture will disappoint you, and an implant-retained overdenture is the honest alternative to discuss. If you have an active gum infection or untreated decay elsewhere, that is treated first.

We will say so at the consultation if a denture is not what your case needs. A prosthodontist assesses the ridges, the remaining bone and the opposing arch before recommending anything.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and assessment, 45 to 60 minutes.** Clinical examination, radiographs, and an assessment of the ridge, the soft tissue and the opposing arch. Any extractions or preliminary treatment are planned here. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Primary impressions, 30 minutes.** A first impression of each arch, from which a custom tray is made to your own anatomy rather than a stock tray.
3. **Definitive impressions, 30 to 45 minutes.** The custom tray records the full denture-bearing area, including the border seal that gives an upper denture its suction.
4. **Jaw relation records, 30 to 45 minutes.** Wax rims establish the height of the bite, the midline, the lip support, and the shade and shape of the teeth. This appointment decides how the finished denture will look.
5. **Wax try-in, 30 to 45 minutes.** The teeth are set in wax so you can see and approve them before the denture is processed. Changes cost nothing at this stage and a great deal after it, so take your time here.
6. **Insertion, 45 to 60 minutes.** The finished denture is fitted, the bite is checked, and pressure points are relieved.
7. **Review and adjustment, two to three short visits over the following month.** New dentures settle. Sore spots are normal in the first fortnight and are relieved by adjustment, not by leaving the denture out.

Most cases run to five or six appointments across four to eight weeks, spaced by the laboratory work between them. Cases involving extractions, an immediate denture or a reline take longer, and the plan you receive at consultation states the sequence for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**iTero intraoral scanner.** Used to capture digital records of the arches and the opposing teeth. A digital record can be re-sent to the laboratory without recalling you for a second impression.

**OccluSense bite analysis.** A pressure-mapping system that shows where and how hard each contact lands, in sequence, on a screen. On a complete denture the bite is the difference between a denture you forget you are wearing and one that rocks, and OccluSense turns that adjustment from a feel into a reading.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. A CBCT scan shows the remaining bone in three dimensions, which matters when the ridge is resorbed, when extractions are planned in the same course of treatment, or when you want to know whether implant retention is realistic later.

Fit is judged over the following month rather than on the day. Tissue settles under a new denture, the bite beds in, and two or three short adjustment visits are part of the treatment rather than a sign that something went wrong.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**How long will I be without teeth?**
If an immediate denture is planned, you are not. It is inserted on the day the teeth are removed. If a conventional denture is planned, the healing period is usually eight to twelve weeks, and that timing is agreed with you before any extraction is booked.

**Will it hurt?**
The denture itself is not a surgical procedure. Any extractions are carried out under local anaesthetic. Sore spots in the first two weeks of wearing a new denture are common and are relieved by adjustment at a short review visit, so book the review rather than leaving the denture out.

**Will I be able to eat normally?**
Not immediately. Start with soft food cut small, chew on both sides at once rather than one side, and reintroduce harder textures over the first few weeks. Most patients notice the largest change during the first month.

**Will it change how I speak?**
Some sounds, particularly s and f, take practice. Reading aloud for a few minutes a day speeds it up, and most people settle within one to two weeks.

**How long does a complete denture last?**
Dentures wear and the ridge underneath keeps changing shape, so a denture is a maintained appliance rather than a one-time purchase. Plan on a reline when the fit loosens and a review at least once a year. Longevity depends on your tissue, your bite and your care of it.

**Do I take it out at night?**
Yes, unless your prosthodontist tells you otherwise. Leaving it out gives the tissue several hours to recover and reduces the risk of denture stomatitis.

**How do I clean it?**
Brush it every day over a basin of water with a denture brush and soap or a denture cleaner, never abrasive toothpaste. Soak it overnight in a denture solution or plain water. Do not use hot water, which distorts acrylic. Clean your gums, tongue and palate with a soft brush each morning.

**Do I still need check-ups if I have no natural teeth?**
Yes. The annual review checks the fit, the bite, the condition of the ridge and the soft tissue, including a screening of the oral mucosa. That examination does not depend on having teeth.
