## §2. What it is

A digital dental X-ray replaces the film that used to be developed in a darkroom with an electronic sensor. The image appears on screen within seconds, the contrast and magnification can be adjusted while you are still in the chair, and the exposure needed is a fraction of what film required. Nothing is developed, so nothing is lost, and the image is stored in your record and can be sent to another clinician if you ask for it.

Several types answer different questions. A periapical image shows one or two teeth from crown to root tip, and is what a clinician looks at for an abscess, a fracture or the length of a root canal. A bitewing shows the contact points between the back teeth, where decay starts out of sight. A panoramic image captures both jaws, the sinuses and the joints in one view, and is used for wisdom teeth, growth assessment and a general overview. A lateral cephalogram shows the profile in one plane and is used in orthodontic planning.

3D CBCT imaging is a different instrument again. It captures a volume rather than a picture, so the anatomy can be examined from any angle and measured in three dimensions. It is available at all four clinics.

## §3. Who it suits

Imaging is taken when a clinical question needs answering: pain of unclear origin, decay suspected between the teeth, an assessment of the bone before periodontal treatment, the root anatomy before root canal treatment, an impacted or unerupted tooth, planning for implants, orthodontic assessment, or a review of work done previously.

3D CBCT imaging is used where the two-dimensional image is not enough. Implant planning is the clearest example, because the height and width of the bone and the position of the nerve canal all have to be measured before a plan exists. Complex root anatomy, suspected fractures, resorption, impacted teeth close to other structures, and joint assessment are the others.

**Where an X-ray is not the right answer.** Radiography is prescribed on clinical need, not on request and not by routine. If nothing in the examination raises a question that an image would answer, no image is taken. CBCT specifically is not a default upgrade: it delivers a higher dose than a single conventional image, so it is used where the extra information changes the treatment and not simply because it is available.

If you are pregnant or think you may be, tell us before any imaging. Elective imaging is deferred, and anything genuinely necessary is discussed with you first. Children are imaged at the minimum needed for the clinical question. If you have had recent radiographs elsewhere, bring them or ask that clinic to send them, and we will use those rather than repeat them.

## §4. How it works

1. **Clinical assessment first, part of your consultation.** The clinician examines you and decides what question needs answering. The image is prescribed to that question, and you are told what is being taken and why before anything is switched on.
2. **Preparation, one to two minutes.** Glasses, earrings and any removable appliance come out, because metal casts a shadow on the image. Protective shielding is used where it is appropriate to the projection.
3. **Positioning, one to three minutes.** For a periapical or bitewing, a small sensor is placed inside your mouth and you hold it steady with a bite block. For a panoramic image or a CBCT scan, you stand or sit at the machine, your head is positioned against a support, and the arm rotates around you.
4. **Exposure, seconds.** A periapical exposure is a fraction of a second. A panoramic rotation takes around ten to twenty seconds. A CBCT scan takes roughly ten to forty seconds depending on the volume captured. You need to stay still for that time, and nothing touches you during it.
5. **Review on screen, five to ten minutes.** The image appears immediately. Your clinician goes through it with you on the monitor, points out what prompted the question and what the image shows, and explains what it means for the plan.
6. **Record and release.** The image is stored in your record. Ask for a copy and you get one, in a standard format you can take to another clinician.

Most conventional imaging is done inside the consultation appointment. A CBCT scan is usually done at the same visit, and the interpretation is reviewed with you before you leave.

## §5. Who performs it

Imaging is not the property of one discipline. Digital dental X-rays and 3D CBCT imaging run underneath all of them, which is why radiography sits as its own section on this site: an endodontist reads a periapical image for canal anatomy, a periodontist reads the same technology for bone level, an orthodontist reads a cephalogram for skeletal relationship, and an implant case is planned on a CBCT volume.

Images are taken by trained clinical staff and interpreted by the specialist responsible for your treatment. The practical consequence is that the scan and the person who acts on it are in the same building: the image does not go to an external imaging centre, and you do not make a second trip to have it read.

## §6. The technology behind it

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics: Mitsukoshi BGC, Shangri-La Plaza, Bonifacio Stopover BGC and Alabang Town Center. Cone beam computed tomography captures a three-dimensional volume of the jaw, so bone height and width can be measured, the position of the nerve canal identified, root anatomy examined from any angle, and an implant position planned before any surgery is booked. Having it in every clinic is the difference between planning your case where you are treated and being sent to an imaging centre and back.

**iTero intraoral scanner.** Not radiography. It captures the surface of the teeth and gums optically, with no radiation at all, and produces the digital model used for aligners, crowns, bridges and dentures. It is often paired with a CBCT volume, so the surface record and the bone record can be aligned in one plan.

**RayFace facial scanner.** Also not radiography. It records the proportions of your actual face in three dimensions, so treatment can be planned against your facial reference points rather than against the teeth in isolation.

Our clinics run eight named systems. These three are the imaging and scanning set.

## §8. Results

The result of an imaging appointment is not a before and an after. It is a finding, an explanation, and a decision you are part of.

You see the image on the monitor at the same appointment, at a size you can actually read, with the clinician pointing to the specific feature that answers the question: the dark shadow between two molars, the widened space at a root tip, the bone level against the root, the position of the nerve canal relative to a planned implant. The image is then stored in your record, so the next one can be compared with it rather than read in isolation. Comparison over time is where radiography earns most of its value, and it only works if the images are kept and matched.

If you want a copy, ask. Your images are your record, and they are released in a standard format on request.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Is dental radiography safe?**
Dental images use a small dose, and digital sensors need a fraction of the exposure film required. That does not make the dose zero, which is why each image is prescribed to a clinical question rather than taken as a matter of routine. Tell us about recent imaging elsewhere so it is not repeated.

**How often do I need X-rays?**
There is no fixed interval that applies to everyone. It depends on your decay risk, your gum condition, your age and what treatment is planned. Some patients need bitewings every year or two, others much less often. Your clinician will tell you the interval that applies to you and why.

**Can I have an X-ray while pregnant?**
Tell us before any imaging is taken. Elective imaging is deferred until after the pregnancy. Where an image is genuinely necessary, for instance to diagnose an infection, it is discussed with you first and taken with appropriate shielding.

**What is the difference between a panoramic image and a CBCT scan?**
A panoramic image is a single flattened picture of both jaws, useful as an overview. A CBCT scan is a three-dimensional volume that can be examined slice by slice from any direction and measured accurately. The overview answers a general question. The volume answers a surgical one.

**Does it hurt?**
No. The sensor for a periapical or bitewing image sits against the inside of the mouth and can feel awkward for a few seconds, particularly at the back. Tell the clinician if it is uncomfortable, because the holder can be repositioned. A panoramic image and a CBCT scan involve nothing touching you at all.

**Can I get a copy of my images?**
Yes. Ask at the appointment or afterwards, and they are released in a standard format you can take to another clinician or keep for your own record.

**Do I need a scan before an implant?**
For implant planning, a 3D volume is what makes an accurate plan possible, because bone height, bone width and the position of the nerve canal have to be measured rather than estimated. Your surgeon will confirm what your case needs at the assessment.

**What is the aftercare?**
There is none. Imaging has no recovery, no anaesthetic and no restriction afterwards. You can eat, drive and go back to work immediately.
