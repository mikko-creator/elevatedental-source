## §2. What it is

Inside every tooth is a chamber and a set of narrow canals running down each root. They contain the pulp, which is nerve tissue and blood vessels. When decay, a crack or an injury lets bacteria reach the pulp, it becomes inflamed and then dies, and the infection travels down the canal and out of the root tip into the bone. That is what causes the throbbing, the swelling and the tenderness to biting.

Root canal treatment removes that tissue. The canals are cleaned along their whole length, shaped so they can be filled, irrigated with disinfecting solutions, then sealed with gutta-percha and a sealer. The access opening is closed, and a back tooth is then restored with a crown, because a tooth that has been hollowed out to reach its canals is at real risk of splitting under chewing force.

What it solves is a straightforward choice: keep the tooth or lose it. Nothing replaces a natural tooth as well as the tooth itself, and root canal treatment is what makes keeping it possible once the pulp is beyond recovery.

## §3. Who it suits

Root canal treatment is indicated when the pulp is irreversibly inflamed or has died. The usual signs are lingering pain to hot or cold that continues after the stimulus is removed, pain on biting, a tooth that has darkened, a swelling or a small bump on the gum near the root, or an area of infection visible on a radiograph at the root tip. It is also carried out on a tooth that has been badly fractured, and as a planned step before certain crown and bridge work when the pulp is already compromised.

Retreatment is a distinct case. A tooth that was root treated years ago and has become symptomatic again can often be reopened, the old filling removed, and the canal system cleaned and sealed properly a second time.

**Where root canal treatment is not the right answer.** A tooth with a vertical root fracture cannot be saved, and cleaning the canal will not change that. A tooth with too little structure left above the gum has nothing to build a restoration on. A tooth with advanced bone loss from gum disease will not become stable because the inside of its root is clean. In those cases the honest recommendation is extraction, followed by a discussion of an implant, a bridge or a partial denture. We would rather tell you that at the assessment than after you have paid for a treatment that was never going to hold.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and diagnosis, 45 to 60 minutes.** Clinical examination, sensitivity and percussion testing, and radiographs. A CBCT scan is taken where the two-dimensional image cannot answer the question. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Anaesthesia and isolation, first 15 minutes of the treatment visit.** The tooth and the surrounding tissue are numbed with local anaesthetic. A rubber dam is placed to isolate the tooth, which keeps saliva and bacteria out of the canals and is the standard of care for this treatment.
3. **Access, 15 to 20 minutes.** A small opening is made through the biting surface, or the back of a front tooth, to reach the pulp chamber.
4. **Cleaning and shaping, 30 to 60 minutes.** Each canal is located, measured to its full working length, cleaned and shaped, and irrigated repeatedly with disinfecting solution. This is the part of the treatment that determines the outcome.
5. **Medication and temporary seal, where a second visit is planned.** An antibacterial dressing is placed in the canals and the tooth is sealed temporarily for one to two weeks.
6. **Filling the canals, 30 to 45 minutes.** The canals are filled with gutta-percha and sealer, and a radiograph confirms the fill reaches the correct length.
7. **Core build-up and definitive restoration, one to two weeks later.** The tooth is rebuilt, and a crown is fitted on back teeth and on any front tooth with substantial structure loss.
8. **Review, at six to twelve months.** A radiograph confirms that the bone at the root tip is healing.

Most cases take one to three visits of 60 to 90 minutes each. Molars have more canals than front teeth and take longer. Curved canals, retreatment cases and teeth with an active abscess take longer still, and the plan you receive at consultation states the sequence for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. A conventional radiograph flattens a three-dimensional root system into one image, which is why a fourth canal in an upper molar, a second canal in a lower incisor, a curve, a resorption defect or a small lesion at the root tip can be missed. A CBCT scan shows the anatomy in three dimensions before treatment starts. It is the single most useful thing you can put in front of a clinician planning a root canal, and it is the reason a difficult case does not have to be sent elsewhere.

**Sol Dental Laser.** Used as an adjunct during disinfection, to help carry irrigating solution into the fine lateral branches of the canal system that instruments cannot reach.

**iTero intraoral scanner.** Used for the restoration that follows. The prepared tooth, the opposing arch and the bite are scanned in the chair, so the crown that protects the treated tooth is made from a digital record rather than a second set of impressions.

## §8. Results

The immediate result is measured by what stops. Pain from an irreversibly inflamed pulp usually settles within a few days of the canals being cleaned, and the tenderness to biting settles as the inflammation at the root tip resolves.

The real result is measured on a radiograph six to twelve months later. Where infection had eaten into the bone at the root tip, that bone fills back in when the canal system has been properly sealed, and the follow-up image is where that is confirmed. This is why the review appointment is part of the treatment rather than an optional extra, and it is the only honest way to tell you whether the tooth has healed.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Does root canal treatment hurt?**
The tooth is numbed with local anaesthetic before any work begins, and it is kept numb throughout. Most of the pain people associate with root canals comes from the infection that brings them in, not from the treatment that resolves it. Expect the tooth to be tender to biting for two to four days afterwards, which is normal and responds to ordinary painkillers taken as directed.

**How many visits will it take?**
One to three, depending on the tooth and whether there is an active infection. Front teeth are commonly completed in a single visit. Molars and abscessed teeth are more often done in two.

**Why do I need a crown afterwards?**
Reaching the canals means removing structure from the middle of the tooth, and a root treated tooth is more brittle than a live one. On a back tooth, a crown holds the remaining walls together against chewing force. A treated tooth that splits usually cannot be saved, so the crown is what protects the treatment you have already paid for.

**Can I just have the tooth taken out instead?**
You can, and sometimes that is the right call. But a gap does not stay a gap: the neighbouring teeth tilt, the opposing tooth drifts, and the bite changes. Replacing an extracted tooth with an implant or a bridge generally costs more than saving it. We will lay out both paths with their costs at the consultation.

**What if it was root treated before and still hurts?**
That is a retreatment case, and it is common. The tooth is reopened, the previous filling material removed, the canal system reassessed on CBCT and cleaned again. Many teeth that were written off can be kept this way.

**Will the tooth go dark?**
A front tooth can discolour after root canal treatment. It is treatable, usually by internal whitening of the tooth or by a crown or veneer. Raise it at the consultation if the tooth is visible when you smile, so it is planned for rather than dealt with afterwards.

**Do I need antibiotics?**
Not routinely. Antibiotics do not reach the inside of a dead canal, which is why cleaning it is the treatment. They are prescribed only where there is spreading infection or systemic involvement.

**What is the aftercare?**
Avoid chewing on the tooth until the definitive restoration is in place, particularly while a temporary filling is holding the access cavity. Take painkillers as directed for the first few days. Brush and floss the tooth normally. Contact the clinic if swelling increases, if the pain worsens after the third day, or if the temporary filling comes out.
