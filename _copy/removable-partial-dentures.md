## §2. What it is

A partial denture is a base carrying replacement teeth, held in place by your remaining natural teeth. Retention comes from clasps that engage those teeth, and support comes from a combination of the teeth and the ridge underneath.

Three constructions are common. An acrylic partial denture has a gum-coloured base and is the quickest to make, which also makes it the usual choice as an interim appliance. A cobalt-chrome partial denture has a cast metal framework, which allows a thinner, more rigid plate and better support from the teeth themselves, so it is generally the more comfortable long-term appliance. A flexible thermoplastic partial denture uses a nylon base with tooth-coloured or gum-coloured retention and avoids visible metal clasps, at the cost of rigidity.

The problem it solves is several gaps that a bridge cannot span and that implants are not going to fill, whether for anatomical, medical or timing reasons. Restoring those spaces returns chewing to that side, stops the remaining teeth drifting into the gaps, and supports the lips and cheeks.

## §3. Who it suits

A partial denture suits you if you are missing several teeth, in more than one part of the arch or in a span too long for a bridge, and you still have sound natural teeth worth keeping. It suits patients who have insufficient bone for implants and do not want grafting. It also works well as an interim appliance while implants integrate, so you are not without teeth during healing.

**Where a partial denture is not the right answer.** If you are missing one tooth between two healthy neighbours, a bridge or an implant is the better treatment and you should not be wearing something removable. If almost no teeth remain in the arch, there is not enough left to clasp onto, and a complete denture or an implant-retained overdenture is the honest alternative. If the remaining teeth are loose from gum disease, clasping a denture to them accelerates their loss, so periodontal treatment comes first and the design is decided afterwards.

A partial denture also asks something of you. It has to come out and be cleaned every day, and the teeth it clasps need more attention than they did before, not less. If that routine is not realistic, say so at the consultation and we will plan around it.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and assessment, 45 to 60 minutes.** Clinical examination, radiographs, and an assessment of the remaining teeth, the bone supporting them and the shape of the ridge. The design and the material are agreed. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Preliminary treatment, where needed.** Fillings, extractions, crowns on teeth that will carry clasps, or gum treatment. Quoted separately and completed before the denture is started.
3. **Primary impressions, 30 minutes.** A first record of each arch, from which a custom tray is made.
4. **Definitive impressions and design, 45 minutes.** The custom tray records the teeth and the ridge accurately. For a cobalt-chrome denture, rests and clasp positions are planned on the model, and small shaping adjustments are made to the natural teeth so the framework seats correctly.
5. **Framework try-in, 30 minutes, chrome cases only.** The metal framework is fitted on its own to confirm it seats fully and passively before any teeth are added.
6. **Tooth try-in, 30 to 45 minutes.** The replacement teeth are set in wax so you can see and approve the position, the shade and the appearance of the clasps before the denture is processed.
7. **Insertion, 45 minutes.** The finished denture is fitted, the bite is checked, the clasps are adjusted, and you are shown how to insert and remove it.
8. **Review and adjustment, one to two short visits over the following month.** Sore spots in the first fortnight are normal and are relieved by adjustment.

Most cases run to four or five appointments across two to six weeks, spaced by the laboratory work between them. Cobalt-chrome cases take longer than acrylic ones, and the plan you receive at consultation states the sequence for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**iTero intraoral scanner.** Used to record the arches, the remaining teeth and the bite digitally. The scan is stored, so the design can be reviewed with you on screen and re-sent to the laboratory without recalling you for another impression.

**OccluSense bite analysis.** A partial denture shares the load between your natural teeth and the ridge, and an uneven bite pushes that load onto the wrong place. OccluSense maps the contacts and their force in sequence, so the adjustment at insertion is made against a reading rather than by feel alone.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. Used to assess the bone supporting the teeth that will carry the clasps, and to answer the implant question properly if you want that comparison before committing to a removable appliance.

## §8. Results

A partial denture is judged at the try-in, while it is still wax. That is where you see the tooth position, the shade and, above all, where the clasps will show when you smile. If a clasp is going to be visible in a position you cannot accept, the design is changed then, not after the appliance is processed.

The second judgement is made over the first month of wearing it. A new partial denture feels bulky, the tongue notices every edge, and one or two pressure points usually need relieving. Those adjustment visits are part of the treatment. Most patients report that the appliance stops being noticeable somewhere in the second or third week.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Will the clasps show?**
It depends on where the gaps are and on the material. On a cobalt-chrome denture the clasps are metal and can be visible on teeth near the front, though the position is planned to hide them where the anatomy allows. A flexible thermoplastic denture avoids visible metal. You see and approve the clasp positions at the try-in before anything is finalised.

**Will it hurt?**
Making the denture is not a surgical procedure and does not require anaesthetic. Any extractions are carried out under local anaesthetic. Sore spots in the first two weeks are common and are relieved by adjustment at a short review, so book the review rather than stopping wearing it.

**How long until I can eat normally?**
Start with soft food cut small and chew on both sides at once. Most patients manage a normal diet within two to four weeks, with hard and sticky foods the last to return.

**Do I wear it at night?**
Usually not. Leaving it out overnight gives the gums and the clasped teeth several hours to recover. Your prosthodontist will tell you if your case is an exception.

**How do I clean it?**
Take it out and brush it every day over a basin of water, using a denture brush with soap or a denture cleaner rather than abrasive toothpaste. Soak it overnight in a denture solution or plain water, never hot water. Then clean your own teeth as normal, paying particular attention to the surfaces the clasps sit on, because that is where plaque collects.

**Will it damage my remaining teeth?**
It does not have to, but it raises the stakes. A clasped tooth holds more plaque and carries more load than it did before. Daily cleaning of those teeth and keeping your recall appointments are what protects them.

**How long does a partial denture last?**
The ridge under it keeps changing shape, so a partial denture is a maintained appliance rather than a one-time purchase. Expect a reline when the fit loosens, adjustments if a tooth is lost or restored, and a review at least once a year.

**Can it be changed if I lose another tooth?**
In many cases yes, a tooth can be added to an existing denture rather than the whole appliance being remade. Bring it in as soon as the tooth is lost so the addition can be assessed.
