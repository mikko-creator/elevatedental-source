## §2 - What it is

Decay is a hole, and a hole does not heal. Once bacteria have dissolved through the enamel into the softer dentine underneath, the cavity keeps widening until the damaged tissue is cut away and the space is sealed. That is what a filling does: it removes the decay and restores the missing structure so the tooth can be loaded again.

We restore with composite resin. Composite is a tooth-colored material made of a resin matrix loaded with fine glass or ceramic particles. It is placed soft, shaped directly in the cavity, and set hard with a curing light in layers of about two millimeters at a time. Because it bonds chemically to the prepared enamel and dentine, less healthy tooth has to be cut away to hold it than an older mechanical filling would require.

Shade is matched to your tooth before the cavity is opened, against a shade guide and in natural light where possible. On a front tooth, more than one shade is often layered so the restoration follows the tooth's own translucency rather than sitting on it as a flat patch.

Most fillings are completed in one appointment.

## §3 - Who it suits

A composite filling is the correct treatment when decay has broken through the enamel but the remaining tooth is still strong enough to support a direct restoration. It also repairs a chipped edge, a worn notch at the gum line, and small cosmetic defects on a front tooth.

**Where a filling is not the right treatment.**

When the cavity is large, the tooth has lost a cusp, or the remaining walls are thin, a filling is being asked to do a crown's job. It will flex under load and the tooth will eventually split. Those cases need an inlay, an onlay or a dental crown, and we will tell you which before we start rather than after the restoration fails.

When decay has reached the pulp, the nerve is already infected. Sealing a filling over an infected nerve traps the problem. That case needs root canal treatment first, then a restoration on top. Persistent throbbing, pain that wakes you at night, or lingering sensitivity to heat are the signs, and imaging confirms it.

When a tooth is cracked rather than decayed, a filling can hold the crack open under bite pressure. Cracked teeth are assessed on their own terms.

Very early decay that has not yet broken the enamel surface does not need cutting at all. It can often be arrested with fluoride treatment and a change of routine, and we will monitor it rather than drill it.

## §4 - How it works

1. **Consultation and diagnosis (10 to 15 minutes).** Examination, and imaging where the cavity sits between teeth or close to the nerve. You are shown what is on the image.
2. **Anesthetic (5 to 10 minutes, including onset).** Local anesthetic is given where the cavity is deep or close to the nerve. Small, shallow cavities are often restored without it, and that is your choice, not an assumption.
3. **Decay removal (5 to 15 minutes).** All softened tissue is removed and the cavity is shaped to hold the restoration. Isolation keeps the field dry, which is what makes the bond hold.
4. **Bonding and layering (10 to 20 minutes).** A bonding agent is applied, then composite is placed and light-cured in layers, each shaped to the tooth's own anatomy rather than flattened.
5. **Bite check and adjustment (about 5 minutes).** Occlusion is checked and adjusted. A restoration that sits even slightly high will ache under load, so this step is not optional.
6. **Finishing and polish (about 5 minutes).** The surface is contoured and polished so it resists stain and does not hold plaque at the margin.

A single-surface filling is typically 30 to 45 minutes in the chair. A larger multi-surface restoration, or several teeth in one sitting, runs longer.

## §6 - The technology behind it

**Digital dental X-ray.** Decay between teeth and under an existing restoration is not visible to the eye. Digital imaging shows it, at a lower dose than film, and on a screen you can see during the appointment.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Installed at all four of our clinics. Used where a deep cavity sits close to the nerve or the root anatomy needs to be understood in three dimensions before the tooth is opened.

**OccluSense bite analysis.** A digital record of where and how hard your teeth meet. It is used to check a new restoration against your real bite rather than by eye alone, which is what stops a filling from sitting high.

**iTero intraoral scanner.** A digital scan of the arch, used to record the tooth before and after treatment and to plan where a restoration is part of a larger case.

We name the systems because a name is checkable. "Advanced technology" is not.

## §8 - Results

A well-placed composite restoration matches the surrounding enamel closely enough that most people cannot identify which tooth was treated, returns full chewing function, and seals the tooth against further decay at that site. Composite restorations are typically expected to serve for several years and are then repaired or replaced as the margin wears; how long yours lasts depends on the size of the cavity, where it sits in the arch, your bite forces and your home care.

A restoration is a repair, not a replacement for the original tooth. Decay can start again at a new site, which is why the six-month recall matters more after a filling, not less.

## §9 - FAQ and closing CTA

**Will it hurt?**
Local anesthetic is used wherever the cavity is deep or near the nerve, so the preparation itself is not felt. You may feel vibration and water. Small shallow cavities are often restored comfortably without anesthetic, and that decision is yours.

**How long does a filling last?**
Several years is the usual expectation for a composite restoration, and small restorations in low-load positions tend to serve longer than large ones on molars. Bite force, grinding, and how well the margin is kept clean all shorten or extend it. Your dentist checks every existing restoration at each recall.

**Can I choose a silver filling instead?**
We restore with tooth-colored composite. If you have an existing amalgam restoration that is sound, it does not need replacing simply because it is visible; if it is failing, we will show you the margin and explain the options.

**Can I eat straight afterwards?**
Composite is fully set by the time you leave, so yes. If you have had local anesthetic, wait until the numbness has gone before eating, because a numb lip and cheek are easy to bite.

**My tooth is sensitive after the filling. Is that normal?**
Short-lived sensitivity to cold and to pressure is common for a few days and sometimes a few weeks after a deep restoration, as the nerve settles. What is not normal is pain that builds, wakes you at night, or lingers after heat. Call us if that happens.

**My bite feels high.**
Come back. It is a two-minute adjustment. A restoration a fraction of a millimeter proud will make the tooth ache under load and can crack it, and it does not settle on its own.

**Do I need an X-ray before a filling?**
Usually yes, where the cavity is between teeth or under an existing restoration, because those surfaces cannot be assessed by eye. You are shown the image and told what it shows.

**Aftercare.**
Wait for numbness to pass before eating. For the first 24 hours, chew on the other side if the restoration is large. Avoid biting directly into very hard food such as ice or bone with a newly restored tooth. Brush and floss the restored tooth normally from the same day: the margin between filling and tooth is where new decay starts, so it needs cleaning, not protecting.
