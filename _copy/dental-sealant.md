## §2. What it is

Look closely at a back tooth and you will see a pattern of grooves running across the chewing surface. On some children those grooves are shallow. On others they are deep, narrow fissures, and the tip of a toothbrush bristle is wider than the opening. Food and bacteria go in. The brush does not follow.

This is why most childhood decay starts on the chewing surfaces of the first permanent molars rather than anywhere else in the mouth.

A sealant closes those grooves. It is a flowable resin, painted into the clean, dry fissure system and hardened with a curing light in seconds. The result is a smooth, sealed surface that a brush can actually clean. Glass ionomer is used instead of resin in some situations, usually where the tooth cannot be kept dry enough for resin to bond, and it releases fluoride as a secondary benefit.

Nothing is drilled and nothing is removed. A sealant is not a filling. A filling repairs damage that has already happened. A sealant is placed on a healthy tooth to prevent the damage in the first place, which is why it is the least invasive item on this entire site.

## §3. Who it suits

Sealants suit children whose permanent molars have come through and have deep grooves. The usual timing is the first permanent molars, which arrive around age six, and the second molars, which arrive around age twelve. Premolars are sealed where the grooves warrant it.

They are worth most in a child who has already had decay, who has deep fissures, who eats or drinks sugar frequently, or who finds thorough brushing difficult. Sealing is also routinely worthwhile for a teenager about to go into fixed braces, because braces make the whole mouth harder to clean for the next two years.

The honest limits are these.

A sealant cannot be placed on a tooth that already has decay reaching into dentine. Sealing over established decay traps it. That tooth needs a filling, and the examination is what tells the difference between a stained groove and a cavity.

A tooth that is only partly erupted cannot be sealed reliably yet. Resin needs a dry field to bond, and a tooth still under a flap of gum cannot be kept dry. The correct answer there is to wait and reappoint, not to place a sealant that will fail in six months. You will be told to come back rather than charged for one now.

Sealants do not protect the surfaces between teeth. Decay between molars is caused by what stays there overnight, and only flossing and diet address it. A sealed tooth is not a sealed mouth, and any clinic implying otherwise is overselling a small, useful procedure.

Baby teeth are sealed only case by case, usually where a child is at high risk and the tooth still has years of service left.

Sealants are also not permanent. They wear, and they chip. They are checked at every recall and repaired or replaced when they need it. That is normal, and it is part of what the recall appointment is for.

## §4. How it works

The whole appointment takes about 10 to 15 minutes per tooth. There is no injection, no drilling and nothing that a child needs to brace for.

**1. Examination, 10 to 15 minutes.** The dentist checks which teeth have erupted, how deep the fissures are, and whether any of them already have decay. Radiographs are taken only where they are needed to see between the teeth. You are told which teeth are candidates today and which should wait.

**2. Cleaning the surface, 2 to 3 minutes per tooth.** The chewing surface is cleaned so the sealant bonds to enamel rather than to a film of plaque.

**3. Isolation and drying, 2 minutes.** Cotton rolls or a rubber dam keep the tooth dry. This step decides whether the sealant lasts, so it is not hurried.

**4. Etching, about 20 seconds.** A mild acid gel is applied to the enamel to give the resin a surface to grip, then rinsed off and the tooth dried again.

**5. Placing the sealant, 1 to 2 minutes.** The resin is flowed into the grooves with a fine applicator so it fills them rather than bridging them.

**6. Curing, 20 to 40 seconds.** A blue curing light hardens the resin. It is set immediately, and your child can eat straight afterwards.

**7. Bite check, 2 minutes.** The sealed surface is checked against the opposing tooth and adjusted if it feels high.

**8. Recall, every 6 months.** Each sealant is inspected for wear and chipping and topped up when required.

## §5. Who performs it

Sealants are placed by a pediatric dentistry specialist. The difference is not manual skill on a two-minute procedure. It is judgement and handling: knowing which groove is stained and which is decayed, knowing when a tooth is not dry enough to seal, and being able to get a nervous six-year-old to sit still without turning the visit into something they will remember badly for the next decade.

If the examination finds decay that needs a filling, or if crowding suggests the orthodontist should look early, those clinicians work in the same practice. Your child is not sent elsewhere for the next step.

## §6. The technology behind it

Sealing a tooth is a low-technology procedure and this page will not pretend otherwise. It needs a clean, dry tooth, a good light and a steady hand. Naming an expensive machine that has nothing to do with it would tell you nothing.

What the named systems in our clinics do here is support the examination and the visit around the sealant.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. It is not used for placing a sealant and your child will not be scanned for one. It is relevant in pediatric dentistry for a different question: locating an unerupted or impacted permanent tooth when one is late arriving.

**AirFlow (guided biofilm therapy).** Our clinics operate AirFlow for professional cleaning. Whether it is used before a sealant is a clinical decision made at the appointment, and it depends on how much plaque is sitting on the surface to be sealed.

**Digital dental radiography.** Where decay between the back teeth needs to be ruled out before sealing, a digital radiograph does it at a low dose and is viewed on screen immediately, so you see what the dentist sees while your child is still in the chair.

**Number of teeth.** Sealants are priced per tooth. Most children have two or four teeth sealed in one visit, and the visit itself is not repeated for each one.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**At what age should my child have sealants?**
Usually soon after the first permanent molars arrive, around age six, and again around age twelve when the second molars come through. The right answer for your child is decided by what has actually erupted, which is what the examination looks at.

**Does it hurt?**
There is no injection and no drilling. Your child will feel the tooth being cleaned and dried, will taste the etching gel briefly, and will need to keep their mouth open for a few minutes. Most children find it one of the easier appointments they will have.

**How long does a sealant last?**
Commonly several years, and it depends on the bite and on how hard your child chews. Sealants wear down gradually rather than failing suddenly, which is why they are inspected at each six-month recall and repaired when needed.

**Are sealants safe?**
Resin sealants have been in routine clinical use for decades and are placed in very small quantities on the outer surface of enamel. If you have specific concerns about materials, raise them at the consultation and you will get a straight answer, including the option of a glass ionomer alternative.

**If my child has sealants, do they still need to brush?**
Yes, and to floss. A sealant protects one surface of one tooth. The surfaces between the teeth and along the gum line are still exposed, and they are where the decay a sealant does not prevent shows up.

**Can adults have sealants?**
Yes, where an adult still has deep, unfilled fissures and a history of decay. It is less common because most adult molars have either been restored already or have proven themselves over decades.

**What if the sealant comes off?**
Bring your child in. A lost or chipped sealant is replaced, and it is a short appointment. It is not a treatment failure, it is wear.

**Do sealants replace fluoride treatment?**
No. They do different jobs. A sealant physically blocks the grooves on the chewing surface. Fluoride hardens the whole enamel surface against acid. Children at higher decay risk usually have both.

**Aftercare.** Your child can eat and drink immediately, though it is sensible to avoid very hard or sticky food on the first day. Keep brushing twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste and start flossing between the back teeth as soon as they touch. Avoid chewing ice and hard candy, which chip sealants off. Keep the six-month recall so each sealant can be checked while a repair is still small.

**Closing.** The first consultation is free. Your child is examined, you are told exactly which teeth would benefit from sealing, which should wait, and which need something else instead, with the figure in writing before anything is done.
