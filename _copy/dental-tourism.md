## §2 - Who this is for

This page is written for two people.

The first lives overseas and is planning a trip around treatment. The second is a Filipino returning home for a defined period and wants the work finished while here. The clinical question is the same for both: does your case fit inside the time you actually have?

**A compressed schedule suits:**

- Cosmetic work with a defined sequence, such as porcelain, zirconia or direct veneers, and crowns.
- Restorative work that can be staged inside one stay, such as fillings, root canal treatment followed by a crown, and scaling and root planing.
- Preventive and hygiene work, including oral prophylaxis and AirFlow (guided biofilm therapy), alongside a larger plan.
- Zoom whitening as a finishing step once restorative work is complete.
- A second assessment on a treatment plan you have been quoted elsewhere, with your own imaging taken here.

**A compressed schedule does not suit:**

- Full orthodontic treatment. Braces and clear aligners run 12 to 24 months and cannot be finished inside a visit.
- Implant cases that require the implant to integrate with bone before the tooth is restored. That happens over months, not days, and is usually planned across two trips.
- Any case where a healing period sits between two stages. The healing period is clinical, not administrative, and it does not compress.

If your case falls in the second list, our specialists will tell you at the assessment stage, before you book anything. That is the point of assessing first.

## §3 - The five-step journey

The programme has five steps, and they run in order.

1. **Consultation and assessment.** Records reviewed remotely, an indicative plan returned in writing.
2. **Smile design.** Your intended result planned on a facial scan and a digital impression.
3. **Travel arrangements.** Dates set around the clinical schedule, coordinated by the Dental Tourism Officer.
4. **Treatment.** The work itself, sequenced with recovery time built in.
5. **Maintenance recall.** Aftercare once you are home, with remote review.

Nothing is skipped to make the trip shorter. Each step exists because the one after it depends on it.

---

## §4 - Step one: consultation and assessment

Before you book a flight, you send records.

What we ask for: clear photographs of your teeth and smile, any existing imaging or reports you hold, your dental history, and a plain description of what you want changed. A specialist in the relevant discipline reviews them.

What you get back: an indicative plan in writing. What is likely needed, in what sequence, roughly how many appointments it takes, and an indicative cost range. You can read it twice, sit on it, and ask questions before committing to a single date.

You do not board a plane on a guess.

One honest limit applies. An indicative plan is built from photographs and history, and photographs do not show what sits under the enamel or inside the bone. Your plan is confirmed in person, after examination and imaging on arrival. Where the confirmed plan differs from the indicative one, you are told before treatment starts, not after.

---

## §5 - Step two: smile design

We use a RayFace facial scanner to plan veneers, crowns and smile design against the proportions of your actual face. A tooth that looks correct in isolation can look wrong in a face. Planning against the face is how that is avoided.

An iTero intraoral scanner records your teeth digitally. There is no putty impression and no tray, and the record can be reviewed and re-sent without you sitting down again.

Together they produce a design you can look at before you commit to it. You see the intended shape, proportion and alignment while there is still time to change your mind.

A design is a plan, not a promise. It shows the intended endpoint and it is what the treatment aims at. Your specialist will tell you which parts of it are settled and which depend on what is found in person.

## §6 - Step three: travel arrangements

The Dental Tourism Officer is one named point of contact for the journey. You are not forwarded between a booking desk, a clinic reception and a treatment coordinator, and you are not repeating your case to a new person each time.

## §7 - Step four: treatment

A trip is planned around the slowest step in your treatment, not the fastest.

**Veneers and crowns.** These need two appointment blocks with laboratory time between them. The gap is real work happening elsewhere, and the trip is built around it rather than compressed through it.

**Root canal treatment with a crown.** The endodontic stage and the restoration are often sequenced within one stay, depending on the tooth and how it responds.

**Implants.** Surgical placement and final restoration are separated by integration with bone. Where that applies to you, your plan is usually split across two trips, and we will say so before you book the first one.

Rest days sit between appointments deliberately. Recovery is part of the schedule, not an interruption to it. You will have swelling on some days, a soft diet on others, and appointments that need you to arrive unhurried.

We do not shorten a plan to make a trip look shorter. A schedule that reads well and treats badly is not a schedule.

## §8 - Step five: maintenance recall

Treatment does not end at the airport.

You leave with written aftercare: what to expect in the first days, what is normal, what is not, and what to do about each. Where a temporary restoration is in place, you leave knowing how long it is meant to last and what it needs.

Remote review follows. You send photographs at agreed intervals and the specialist who treated you reviews them, so the case stays with the person who planned it.

If something needs attention while you are abroad, contact the clinic first. We can send your records, imaging and treatment notes to a dentist where you are, so whoever sees you is working from the actual case rather than from your description of it. What we cannot do is treat you from here, and we will not pretend otherwise.

## §9 - Your specialists

Your case is assigned to the specialist whose discipline it belongs to.

An orthodontist plans orthodontics. A prosthodontist plans crowns, bridges and full-mouth restoration. An endodontist performs the root canal treatment. An oral surgeon places the implant. A periodontist treats the gum and bone that the rest of it depends on.

They practise together. A complex case is not handed to another clinic in the middle of your stay, and you are not managing a referral in a country you do not live in. For an international patient this is the part that matters most, because a referral abroad costs a flight, not a taxi.

Every specialist is published with their discipline and their credentials, so you know who is treating you before you travel.

Your schedule will have gaps in it, and the gaps are deliberate. Healing time is not dead time.

Where an experience is arranged around your treatment, it is scheduled on a day your clinical plan can carry it, and never on a day you should be resting.
