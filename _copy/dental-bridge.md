## §2. What it is

A bridge is one piece of dental work made of several joined units. The false tooth in the middle is the pontic. The units on either side are crowns that sit over your own teeth, and those teeth carry the load. The whole span is cemented as a single restoration.

Material decides both the look and the strength. Zirconia is the usual choice where chewing force is highest, at the back of the mouth. Lithium disilicate, sold as Emax, is used at the front where translucency matters and the light has to pass through the tooth the way enamel does. Porcelain fused to metal remains a durable option in specific cases. Your prosthodontist will tell you which material the case calls for and why, before the tooth is prepared.

The problem a bridge solves is a gap that has begun to affect the rest of the mouth. Teeth on either side of a space tilt into it, the opposing tooth drifts down, food packs into the gap, and the bite changes. Closing the space stops that sequence and restores chewing on that side.

## §3. Who it suits

A bridge suits you if you are missing one tooth, or a short run of teeth, and the teeth on both sides of the gap are healthy, well supported by bone, and either already crowned or heavily restored. If those neighbours already need crowns, a bridge does two jobs at once.

It also suits patients who want a fixed replacement without surgery, or who cannot have an implant placed for medical or anatomical reasons.

**Where a bridge is not the right answer.** A bridge requires the neighbouring teeth to be reduced in size to take the crowns. If those teeth are sound, untouched and free of fillings, a single implant is usually the better treatment, because it replaces the missing tooth without altering the healthy ones. If the supporting teeth have significant bone loss from gum disease, they cannot carry the extra load, and periodontal treatment comes first. If the gap is long, a bridge across it will flex and fail, and an implant-supported solution or a removable partial denture is the honest alternative.

We will say so at the consultation if a bridge is not what your case needs.

## §4. How it works

1. **Consultation and assessment, 45 to 60 minutes.** Clinical examination, radiographs, and an assessment of the supporting teeth and the bone around them. Material and design are agreed. You receive the written quote at this appointment.
2. **Preliminary treatment, where needed.** Decay, failing fillings, root canal treatment or gum treatment on the supporting teeth is completed before the bridge is started. This is quoted separately.
3. **Preparation and records, 90 to 120 minutes.** The supporting teeth are numbed and shaped to receive the crowns. The arch is scanned or impressed, the bite is recorded, and the shade is matched.
4. **Temporary bridge, same appointment.** A provisional bridge is fitted so the prepared teeth are protected and the gap is not visible while the laboratory works.
5. **Laboratory fabrication, one to two weeks.** The bridge is built to the scan and the bite record.
6. **Try-in and cementation, 45 to 60 minutes.** Fit, contact points, shade and bite are checked before anything is cemented. Adjustments are made at this stage, not after.
7. **Review, one short visit two to four weeks later.** The bite is rechecked once you have been using it, and you are shown how to clean under the pontic.

Most cases complete in two to three appointments across two to four weeks. Cases needing root canal treatment or gum treatment first take longer, and the plan you receive at consultation states the sequence for your case specifically.

## §6. The technology behind it

**iTero intraoral scanner.** A digital scan replaces the impression tray for most bridge cases. It records the preparations, the opposing arch and the bite in one sitting, and the file goes to the laboratory the same day.

**OccluSense bite analysis.** A bridge splints several teeth together, so a single high contact is carried by the whole span. OccluSense maps where each contact lands and how hard, in sequence, so the bite is adjusted against a reading rather than against a piece of articulating paper alone.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. A CBCT scan shows the bone around the supporting teeth and the root anatomy in three dimensions. It is how we check that the teeth being asked to carry a bridge can actually carry it, and it is how the implant alternative is assessed if you want that comparison before deciding.

## §8. Results

The check that matters happens before cementation, not after it. At the try-in, the fit at the margins, the contact points against the adjacent teeth, the shade in daylight and the bite are all assessed while the bridge can still be sent back. A bridge that is adjusted at try-in and a bridge that is adjusted after cementation are two different standards of work.

The second check is at the review visit. Once you have chewed on it for a fortnight, the bite is read again, because a restoration that felt correct in the chair can settle differently in use.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**Does the neighbouring tooth have to be cut down?**
Yes, for a conventional bridge. The supporting teeth are reduced so the crowns fit over them without making the tooth bulkier than it was. This is the main reason we discuss the implant option with you first when the neighbours are untouched.

**Will it hurt?**
The preparation appointment is carried out under local anaesthetic. The teeth are commonly sensitive to cold for a few days afterwards while the temporary bridge is in place, and that settles once the definitive bridge is cemented. Tell your clinician if it does not.

**How long will I have a temporary bridge?**
One to two weeks in most cases. The temporary looks and works well enough to eat and speak normally, but it is cemented lightly on purpose so it can be removed. Avoid very hard or sticky food while it is in.

**How long does a bridge last?**
It depends on the bite, the health of the supporting teeth and how well the area under the pontic is cleaned. A bridge fails at the supporting teeth far more often than at the porcelain, which is why the cleaning routine below matters more than the material.

**How do I clean under it?**
You cannot floss between the units, because they are joined. Use a floss threader or superfloss to pass floss under the pontic once a day, and an interdental brush where the space allows. Two minutes a day here is what keeps the supporting teeth healthy.

**Can a bridge be whitened?**
No. Porcelain and zirconia do not respond to whitening agents. If you are considering whitening your natural teeth, do it first and let the shade settle, then match the bridge to the result.

**What happens if a supporting tooth fails later?**
The bridge has to be removed, the tooth treated or replaced, and the span redesigned. This is why we assess the supporting teeth carefully at the start rather than working around a doubtful one.

**Bridge or implant?**
A bridge is faster and involves no surgery, but it commits the neighbouring teeth. An implant takes longer and involves a surgical stage, but it leaves the neighbours untouched. Both are available in-house, so the recommendation you get is the one your case supports rather than the one the practice happens to offer.
