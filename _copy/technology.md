## §3. 3D CBCT Imaging

Cone beam computed tomography produces a three-dimensional image of your teeth, jawbone, nerve canals and sinuses. A conventional dental radiograph compresses all of that into a single flat plane, where structures overlap and depth disappears. A CBCT scan does not. It can be rotated, sliced and measured.

It changes three things in particular.

**Implant planning.** Before an implant is placed, the surgeon needs the true height and width of the available bone and the exact position of the inferior alveolar nerve. On a flat film those are estimates. On a CBCT scan they are measurements, taken before anything is drilled.

**Oral surgery.** The roots of a lower wisdom tooth can sit directly against that same nerve. Knowing the relationship in three dimensions before the extraction changes the technique chosen, and it changes what you are told to expect afterwards.

**Endodontics.** A root canal treatment that fails often fails because a canal was never found. Extra canals and sharply curved canals that do not show on a flat radiograph do show on a 3D scan, which is why difficult and repeat cases are planned this way.

**3D CBCT imaging is available at all four clinics:** Mitsukoshi BGC, Shangri-La Plaza, Bonifacio Stopover BGC, and Alabang Town Center. You are not sent to one site for the scan and back to another for the treatment, and the scan is read by the specialist who will do the work.

## §4. iTero and RayFace Scanning

**iTero intraoral scanner.**

A handheld wand captures a high-resolution digital impression of your teeth in minutes. There is no tray, no impression putty setting in your mouth, and no gag reflex to manage. For many patients this is the most noticeable difference between a conventional impression and a digital one.

The output is a file, and that is the part that matters clinically. It can reach the laboratory the same day instead of traveling as a physical model. It is stored as a baseline, so a later scan can be laid over it to show exactly how far a tooth has moved or a gum has receded. Clear aligner planning, crowns and veneer cases all begin here.

**RayFace facial scanner.**

RayFace scans your face in three dimensions, not only your teeth. Veneers and smile design are then planned against your own facial proportions: the midline of your face, the line your lips take when you smile, the width of the smile against the width of the face, the way the upper teeth sit relative to your lower lip.

This is the difference between a set of teeth that is correct on a model and a set of teeth that looks right on you. Both are possible outcomes of the same laboratory work. Planning on a facial scan is how the second one is aimed at deliberately instead of hoped for.

## §5. OccluSense Bite Analysis

Articulating paper marks where your teeth touch. It cannot tell anyone how hard, or in what order.

OccluSense records both. It captures the force and the timing of every contact digitally, so your occlusion is read as data across the whole arch rather than interpreted from smudges of ink.

That matters most after restorative work. A crown, a veneer or a finished aligner case that sits a fraction high is felt as a high spot, and over the following months it presents as sensitivity, a chipped or cracked restoration, or ache in the jaw joint. Digital occlusal analysis is how the work is checked to function, and not only to look correct, before you leave the clinic with it.

## §6. Sol Laser and Piezotome

**Sol Dental Laser.**

The Sol dental laser handles soft-tissue work precisely: contouring the gum before veneers are fitted, evening an irregular gum line, preparing tissue around a tooth about to take a crown.

The laser cuts and seals as it goes, which means less bleeding during the appointment and, in many cases, no sutures afterwards. Where the case allows it, a shorter procedure replaces a scalpel one.

**Piezotome.**

Piezotome is ultrasonic bone instrumentation. The tip vibrates at a frequency tuned to cut mineralized bone while leaving soft tissue intact, so nerves, sinus membranes and gum are protected while the bone around them is worked.

It is used in surgical extractions, in bone grafting, and in preparing an implant site. The practical difference to you is a more controlled procedure and, in most cases, less swelling in the days that follow. Anesthesia is used as it would be for any surgical appointment, and your surgeon will tell you what recovery is likely to involve in your specific case.

## §7. AirFlow and Zoom

**AirFlow (guided biofilm therapy).**

A controlled jet of warm water, air and fine powder lifts biofilm and stain off the tooth surface, including just below the gum line and around the places a hand instrument struggles to reach: orthodontic brackets, implant abutments and aligner attachments. It is gentler on enamel and on exposed, sensitive tooth necks than scraping, and many patients find the appointment more comfortable than conventional scaling.

**Zoom whitening.**

Light-activated whitening carried out in the clinic in a single session, rather than over weeks of wearing trays at home. A protective barrier is placed over your gums, the gel is applied in timed cycles, and the light activates it between them.

Sensitivity is the common side effect, and the session includes steps taken to limit it. How far the shade moves depends on your starting shade and on what caused the discoloration in the first place, so your dentist will tell you what is realistic for your teeth before the session begins rather than afterwards.
