## §2. What it is

Decay is a chemical process before it is a hole. Bacteria in plaque turn sugar into acid, the acid pulls mineral out of the enamel surface, and if that continues faster than saliva can put mineral back, the surface eventually collapses into a cavity.

Fluoride changes the chemistry. When it is present at the enamel surface it is incorporated into the crystal structure as fluorapatite, which dissolves at a lower pH than the mineral it replaces. In plain terms, the enamel becomes harder to dissolve. Fluoride also slows the bacteria's acid production and helps drive mineral back into an early lesion, which is why a white spot caught early can remineralise rather than progress.

A professional application is a different thing from toothpaste. Toothpaste is a low concentration used twice a day. A varnish applied in clinic is a high concentration held against the tooth for hours: a 5 percent sodium fluoride varnish carries roughly 22,600 parts per million fluoride, against about 1,000 to 1,500 in a family toothpaste. The varnish sets on contact with saliva and stays put while the tooth takes up fluoride, which is the reason the instruction afterwards is to leave it alone.

Gel and foam applied in a tray are the alternative delivery. Varnish is generally preferred in young children because a smaller volume is used and less is swallowed.

## §3. Who it suits

Fluoride treatment is worth most where decay risk is genuinely elevated, and that is the assessment your first appointment does.

It suits a child who has already had decay, because past decay is the strongest predictor of future decay. It suits a child with deep grooves, crowded teeth, or enamel defects. It suits anyone in fixed braces, because brackets hold plaque against the enamel for the length of treatment and the white marks that leaves are permanent. It suits patients with a dry mouth, whether from medication, a medical condition or radiotherapy, because saliva is the body's own remineralising system and without it the balance shifts sharply. It suits adults with exposed root surfaces from gum recession, since root surface is softer than enamel and decays faster. And it helps with the sensitivity that goes with those exposed roots.

The honest limits are these.

Fluoride does not repair a cavity. Once decay has broken through the enamel into the dentine, the tissue is gone and no amount of varnish brings it back. That tooth needs a filling. Fluoride works on the surface and on early lesions, not on established holes.

Fluoride does not replace brushing, flossing or controlling how often sugar is eaten. A child who snacks on sweet drinks all afternoon will out-produce any varnish. If your consultation identifies frequency of sugar as the real problem, you will be told that plainly, because paying for a fluoride application every three months while nothing else changes is money spent to no purpose.

Very young children swallow some of what is applied, and excess fluoride during the years the permanent teeth are forming can cause fluorosis, which is a cosmetic mottling of enamel. Professional application is dose-controlled and applied in small quantities for exactly this reason. Tell us what fluoride your child is already getting, including toothpaste strength, mouthrinse and any supplement, so the total is accounted for rather than guessed at.

If your child has a known allergy to colophony or any resin, say so before the appointment, because some varnishes contain it and an alternative is used.

## §4. How it works

Fluoride application is short. It is normally done at the end of a check-up or a cleaning appointment and adds about 5 to 10 minutes to the visit.

**1. Assessment, part of the examination.** Decay history, diet, brushing, medical history, existing fluoride exposure, and any early white-spot lesions are recorded. This produces a decay risk level, and that level decides the interval, not a standard schedule applied to everyone.

**2. Cleaning, 10 to 30 minutes if it is included.** Fluoride is applied to a clean surface. Where a professional cleaning is part of the same visit, it comes first.

**3. Drying and isolation, 1 to 2 minutes.** The teeth are dried with air and cotton rolls. Varnish adheres better to a dry surface.

**4. Application, 2 to 3 minutes.** The varnish is painted onto all surfaces with a small brush, including between the teeth where the applicator can reach. There is no injection and nothing that has to be held in the mouth. A gel or foam in a tray, if that is used instead, is held for about four minutes.

**5. Setting.** The varnish hardens on contact with saliva within seconds. The teeth may look slightly dull or yellow for a few hours. That is the varnish, not the tooth, and it brushes off the next day.

**6. Instructions, 2 minutes.** No hot drinks, no crunchy or sticky food, and no brushing until the following morning. The longer the varnish stays in contact, the more fluoride is taken up.

**7. Recall.** Commonly every six months. Every three months where risk is high, and that decision is reviewed each time rather than left running indefinitely.

## §6. The technology behind it

A fluoride application uses a brush and a dose of varnish. There is no machine involved in it, and this page will not attach one to it in order to sound more advanced.

What the named systems in our clinics do is support the visit the fluoride belongs to.

**AirFlow (guided biofilm therapy).** Our clinics operate AirFlow for professional cleaning. Fluoride is applied to a clean tooth surface, so where a cleaning appointment is part of the same visit, this is the system that may be used for it.

**Digital dental radiography.** Early decay between the back teeth is invisible to the eye. A digital radiograph, taken only where it is indicated, is what distinguishes a lesion that fluoride can still arrest from one that has passed the point where a filling is required. That distinction is the difference between the correct treatment and a wasted one.

**3D CBCT imaging.** Available at all four clinics. It has no role in a fluoride appointment, and your child will not be scanned for one. In pediatric dentistry it is used for a different question, such as locating an unerupted or impacted permanent tooth.

## §9. FAQ and closing CTA

**How often does my child need it?**
Commonly every six months, alongside the check-up. Every three months where decay risk is high, which usually means recent decay, braces, dry mouth or enamel defects. The interval is set from the risk assessment and reviewed at each visit, so it is not a permanent commitment.

**Does it hurt?**
No injection and no instrument on the tooth. Your child will feel a small brush and will taste the varnish. The most common complaint is the texture on the teeth for a few hours afterwards.

**What can my child eat afterwards?**
Soft, cool food for the next few hours. Avoid hot drinks, crunchy food and anything sticky. Do not brush until the following morning, so the varnish stays in contact overnight. Normal eating resumes the next day.

**Will their teeth look yellow?**
The varnish gives a dull or slightly yellow film for a few hours. It is the coating, not a stain on the tooth, and it comes away with the first brushing the next morning.

**Is fluoride safe for children?**
In the doses used professionally, yes, and it is applied in small, measured quantities specifically to limit how much is swallowed. The concern with fluoride is total intake during the years permanent teeth are forming, so tell us what your child is already using at home. If you would prefer not to have fluoride applied, say so and the alternative preventive measures will be set out for you honestly, including what they do and do not achieve.

**Can fluoride reverse a cavity?**
It can arrest and remineralise an early lesion that has not yet broken through the surface. It cannot rebuild a tooth once decay has entered the dentine. The radiograph and the examination are what tell you which of the two you are dealing with.

**Do adults need fluoride treatment?**
Some do. Exposed root surfaces from gum recession, a dry mouth from medication, orthodontic treatment, or a run of new decay in adulthood are all reasons for professional application. It is not a childhood-only measure.

**Is this the same as fluoride in toothpaste?**
No. The concentration in a professional varnish is many times higher and it stays on the tooth for hours rather than seconds. It works alongside daily fluoride toothpaste rather than replacing it.

**Aftercare.** No hot drinks and no crunchy or sticky food for the rest of the day. No brushing or flossing until the next morning. From the following day, brush twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste, spit rather than rinse so some fluoride remains, and floss between any teeth that touch. Reduce how often sugar is eaten rather than only how much, because frequency is what drives the acid cycle. Keep the recall interval you have been given.
